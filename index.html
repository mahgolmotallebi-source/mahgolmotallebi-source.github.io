My Story

My path into user research and strategic design has crossed architecture, urban sociology, entrepreneurship, and corporate innovation. What connects these fields is a lasting interest in how people navigate complex environments, make decisions, and build relationships with the systems and products around them.

I began in architecture, where I learned to think spatially, systemically, and across different scales. During my Master's in Sustainable Design, I became increasingly interested not only in what we design, but in how design decisions influence people's everyday experiences and behaviour.

That interest led me to a PhD in Urban Sociology, where I studied immigrant belonging and socio-spatial practices in Berlin. Through interviews, fieldwork, and qualitative analysis, I learned how to work with ambiguity, recognise patterns across individual experiences, and translate complex human stories into frameworks and arguments.

During the pandemic, I also built Midtage: Midcentury + Vintage, an online vintage furniture business. Running it gave me direct experience of customer behaviour, brand trust, pricing, logistics, and the everyday decisions involved in turning an idea into a functioning service.

Today

As a Strategic Designer and User Researcher, I bring these perspectives together. I combine the systemic thinking of architecture, the depth of social research, and the practical mindset of entrepreneurship to help teams understand unclear problems and turn evidence into product, service, and strategy decisions.

When I'm not working

I am usually travelling, exploring cities through their food, architecture, design, and everyday local life. It is often another form of observation: noticing how people use places, adapt systems, and make environments their own.

Venture Building

From a broad building-maintenance challenge to a validated product-service concept for early roof-damage detection.

Challenge & rationale

Roof leakage is often detected too late — after moisture has already damaged insulation, materials, or interior spaces. For building owners, facility managers, roofers, and insurers, this creates avoidable repair costs, material waste, and operational disruption. The challenge was to understand whether early roof-damage detection could create meaningful value across this stakeholder ecosystem — and whether the opportunity was desirable, feasible, viable, and strategically relevant for Henkel.

Proposed solution

The proposed solution combined a sensor-integrated roof layer with continuous monitoring, enabling earlier detection and more precise localisation of water damage before it developed into a larger repair problem.

Research-driven strategic vision framework

From the beginning of the venture, my role was to lead the desirability workstream and help the team evaluate whether the opportunity was worth pursuing. We used a four-part strategic lens — desirability, feasibility, viability, and Henkel fit — to connect user needs, technical possibilities, business potential, and organizational relevance.

01

User and stakeholder research: understanding the roofing ecosystem

I conducted 70+ interviews with experts, users, and stakeholders to understand how roof leakage was detected, who carried the risk and cost, and how responsibilities and decisions were distributed across the ecosystem.

I translated the findings into hypotheses, opportunity areas, and research questions that guided concept development and feature priorities.

02

Ideation workshops: turning research insights into solution directions

I designed and facilitated ideation workshops with both business units, using the research findings as prompts for developing potential solutions. We generated more than 200 ideas, grouped them into opportunity areas, and prioritised the directions that best addressed stakeholder needs and business potential.

03

Idea selection: tangible and testable concepts

During the idea-selection phase, I translated the strongest ideas into three distinct concept directions that could be discussed and tested with internal and external stakeholders.

The concepts explored different levels of intervention: a holistic water-damage service, a prevention and emergency toolkit, and a sensor-based roof-monitoring system. Testing these alternatives helped the team compare their relevance and focus the venture on the RoofUmbrella direction.

04

Iterative design and validation: testing product value

After selecting the RoofUmbrella direction, I designed further interviews and exercises to test and refine its value proposition. Stakeholders ranked potential benefits from essential to nice-to-have, helping the team distinguish meaningful value from internal assumptions.

The findings clarified which benefits mattered most and gave the team a more focused basis for product messaging and feature decisions.

05

Mapping the journey across the product lifecycle

I mapped the full roof lifecycle to understand where problems emerged — from installation and use to maintenance, repair, and end-of-life.

The journey helped the team identify recurring pain points, unresolved questions, and possible intervention points. We used these findings to shape hypotheses and define the research priorities for the following sprint.

06

Service blueprint: an end-to-end view of the experience

Building on the lifecycle mapping, I developed the service blueprint to show how the future solution would work across user interactions, digital touchpoints, support activities, and internal operations.

The blueprint helped the team align on the intended experience, clarify responsibilities, and identify what needed to happen behind the scenes for the service to work consistently.

07

Prototyping the physical system and dashboard experience

During prototyping, I worked with an external hardware-design agency, internal specialists, and a research institute to keep technical development connected to the user and service requirements established through research.

Alongside supporting experiment planning and translating findings between partners, I designed an initial dashboard concept for building and facility-management teams. The dashboard integrated roof monitoring into the wider building-management system, showing active leak alerts, the precise location of detected moisture, sensor status, and the information needed to support maintenance decisions.

I reviewed and refined the dashboard concept with relevant stakeholders to assess whether the information, alert hierarchy, and roof map view were clear and actionable. Their feedback helped refine how leak severity, location, sensor readings, and next steps were communicated.

08

Visual communication and animation

To align internal and external stakeholders around the final concept, I translated the product and service into a clear visual story — showing how the sensor layer, hardware, data analysis, dashboard, and leak alerts worked together. I also initiated and directed a product animation that made the concept easier to understand, discuss, and present across the organisation.

09

Outcome

The venture progressed from a broad building-maintenance opportunity to a defined and tested product-service concept, supported by stakeholder research, value-proposition validation, lifecycle mapping, a service blueprint, technical experiments, an initial dashboard concept, and an early physical prototype.

The project reached an early prototype stage, supported by validated stakeholder needs and value propositions, before being paused for strategic reasons. The work gave the team a clearer understanding of the service requirements and the technical and organisational conditions needed to take the concept forward.

The RC50 was an industrial dispensing product with new IoT-enabled features, but the team needed clearer evidence of whether customers understood, valued, and would pay for the digital functionality.

Problem to solve

The product team had introduced new IoT capabilities, but adoption remained uncertain. Sales teams found the digital value difficult to explain, customers were not consistently engaging with the features, and the preferred pricing model was unclear.

The project needed to understand what was blocking adoption, which benefits customers valued most, and how the digital features could be positioned and monetised more effectively.

My contribution

I designed and led a two-phase research programme. First, I surveyed sales and distributor teams to identify communication gaps, customer objections, and barriers in the sales journey. I then designed a customer survey to evaluate feature relevance, IoT readiness, and willingness to pay.

I synthesised both research streams into go-to-market recommendations covering value communication, sales enablement, feature positioning, and monetisation.

01

Understanding adoption barriers through sales and distributor research

I began by researching the perspective of sales and distributor teams, who were closest to customer objections and purchasing decisions. I designed a survey to understand where the IoT-enabled features were difficult to explain, which concerns customers raised, and what was preventing the digital functionality from becoming part of the sales conversation.

I synthesised the findings through root-cause analysis and customer-journey mapping. This helped the team distinguish surface-level symptoms, such as limited customer interest, from underlying issues including unclear value communication, low confidence in the digital features, and insufficient support during key stages of the sales journey.

The analysis showed that the challenge was not only feature adoption; the product's digital value was not being communicated clearly enough for either sales teams or customers.

02

Sales research revealed gaps in value communication and adoption support

The research showed that the IoT features were difficult for sales and distributor teams to explain in a clear and convincing way. Customer interest was limited, active use of the digital functionality remained low, and the value of the features was not always connected to concrete operational benefits.

The findings pointed to three main needs: clearer value communication, stronger sales-enablement materials, and more guidance for customers — to improve understanding and support more effective sales conversations.

These insights helped shift the conversation from simply promoting the IoT functionality to explaining where it could reduce uncertainty, support process visibility, and create practical value in day-to-day operations.

Based on these findings, the next research phase focused directly on customers: which features they valued, how ready they were to adopt connected functionality, and whether they were willing to pay for it.

03

Testing customer value, IoT readiness, and willingness to pay

To complement the sales research, I designed a customer survey for RC50 users to understand how they experienced the existing product, which IoT-enabled features they considered valuable, and what might encourage or prevent adoption.

The survey was structured around three areas: current product usability, readiness to engage with connected functionality, and the perceived value of future IoT features. It also explored willingness to pay, helping the team assess whether the digital functionality should be included, bundled, or offered as a separate service.

By comparing feature relevance, adoption barriers, and pricing expectations, the research provided a stronger basis for product positioning and monetisation decisions.

The findings showed that customers were more receptive to the digital features when they were linked to concrete operational benefits rather than presented as technology alone. They also helped clarify which features carried the strongest perceived value and where willingness to pay remained limited or dependent on clearer proof of return.

04

Translating research into go-to-market action

I combined the sales and customer findings into a set of go-to-market recommendations covering value communication, sales enablement, feature positioning, onboarding, and pricing.

The strategy focused on connecting the IoT features to concrete operational benefits, equipping sales teams with clearer messaging and support materials, and reducing uncertainty for customers during evaluation and early use.

The research gave the team a clearer understanding of why the digital features were difficult to adopt and sell. It also provided a stronger basis for improving ROI communication, refining the product's positioning, and deciding how the digital functionality could be packaged and monetised more effectively.

Smart Wound Care was a joint medtech venture exploring how sensor-integrated wound dressings could help identify infection risk between clinical visits.

Problem to solve

Wound infections can develop between clinical visits and may remain unnoticed until complications appear. This is especially challenging for patients managing complex care routines at home and for healthcare teams with limited visibility between appointments. The project needed to understand where earlier infection signals could provide meaningful support, which patient group would benefit most, and what information would help patients and clinicians take timely action.

Proposed solution

The proposed concept combined a wound dressing with integrated sensors that monitor changes associated with infection risk. The aim was to provide patients and care teams with earlier signals to support more timely assessment and intervention.

01

Identifying the most relevant patient group

I joined the venture during concept validation to help determine where the smart dressing could provide the most meaningful value. While the feasibility team explored the technical concept, I led research into potential wound types, patient groups, and care contexts.

I interviewed two wound-care nurses, three patients living with chronic wounds, and two specialist doctors. The findings highlighted diabetic foot ulcers as the strongest use case because of the risk of complications, challenges in monitoring wounds between visits, and the importance of supporting patients in day-to-day care.

The research also identified early product requirements, including ease of application, clear status information, and the potential relevance of pH monitoring.

02

Quantifying patient needs and willingness to use the concept

To build on the qualitative findings, I designed a survey with 261 people living with diabetic foot ulcers. The research explored their wound-care routines, emotional experience, self-tracking habits, willingness to use a smart dressing, and how they might respond to alerts.

The results showed that many patients already played an active role in changing and monitoring their dressings. They also highlighted a strong need for clearer wound-status information, practical guidance, and support between clinical visits.

Based on these findings, I recommended prioritising wound-status alerts, educational guidance, and features that support patient autonomy and timely action.

These findings shifted the concept beyond infection detection alone. The product also needed to help patients understand what was happening, know when to act, and feel more supported in managing their wound between appointments.

03

Developing behavioural archetypes to guide different forms of support

The survey showed that patients experienced wound care very differently. To help the team design for this variation, I developed four behavioural archetypes based on patients' attitudes, motivations, support needs, and engagement in their care.

The archetypes ranged from patients who struggled to recognise or respond to risks to those who actively monitored and managed their condition. Rather than treating diabetic foot ulcer patients as one homogeneous group, the framework helped us understand what different patients might need to take timely action.

I translated each archetype into a set of product and service implications, including critical alerts, educational guidance, self-reporting tools, peer support, and different levels of intervention. This gave the team a clearer basis for designing support around patient needs rather than assuming one experience would work for everyone.

04

Using journey mapping to understand everyday care challenges

To bring the archetypes into everyday context, I mapped their wound-care journeys across routines such as dressing changes, work, travel, and preparing for bed.

The journey maps helped the team see where patients might feel uncertain, overwhelmed, or unsupported, and where timely information or guidance could make care easier to manage.

These insights translated the archetypes into concrete product opportunities, including clearer wound-status information, reminders, educational support, and alerts linked to moments when patients were most likely to need them.

Outcome

The research helped the team focus the venture on diabetic foot ulcer care, identify the patient needs most relevant to the concept, and translate those findings into product requirements, behavioural archetypes, and journey-based opportunities for support.

The project was paused during prototyping following strategic reprioritisation. Although it did not progress to launch, the research provided a clear evidence base for future patient-centred product development.
